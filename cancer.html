01 / THE PROBLEM

A patient tests positive (⊕)

A lab test returns one of two outcomes: ⊕ (positive) or ⊖ (negative). A new patient’s lab test comes back positive (⊕). Should the patient be diagnosed with cancer or not?

Given probabilities

0.009
0.9% prior prevalence
0.98
98% true positive rate (sensitivity)
0.97
97% true negative rate (specificity)

Drag the sliders to explore how prevalence, sensitivity, and specificity shift the diagnosis.

02 / COMPLEMENT PROBABILITIES

Fill in the missing probabilities

Because each pair of outcomes covers all possibilities, we can derive the prior and likelihood for the healthy hypothesis directly.

P(¬cancer) = 1 − P(cancer)
0.991
1 − 0.009 = 0.991 (99.1% healthy)
P(⊕ | ¬cancer) = 1 − P(⊖ | ¬cancer)
0.03
1 − 0.97 = 0.03 (3% false positive rate)
This gives us everything needed: the prior and likelihood for both hypotheses.
03 / MAP SIMPLIFICATION

Compare the numerators first

Applying Bayes’ rule to a hypothesis h given positive evidence ⊕ gives P(h | ⊕) = P(⊕ | h) P(h) / P(⊕). Since P(⊕) is identical for both hypotheses, MAP compares the numerators directly.

P(h | ⊕) ∝ P(⊕ | h) × P(h)Unnormalized score = likelihood × prior
Applied to cancer
0.00882
0.98 × 0.009 = 0.00882
Applied to ¬cancer
0.02973
0.03 × 0.991 = 0.02973
04 / NORMALIZED POSTERIORS

Compute the actual probabilities

To turn those scores into true probabilities that sum to 100%, divide each numerator by the marginal probability P(⊕)—the total probability of getting a positive test.

P(⊕) = 0.00882 + 0.02973 = 0.03855Total probability of observing a positive test result
P(cancer | ⊕)
0.00882 / 0.03855

05 / THE BASE-RATE EFFECT

Why this is counterintuitive—but correct

The test’s 98% sensitivity sounds very reliable, but because cancer’s base prevalence (0.9%) is so low, the much larger pool of healthy patients produces more false positives (3% of 99.1% of patients) than the small cancer-positive pool produces true positives (98% of 0.9% of patients).

06 / TAKEAWAY

From single evidence to many features

Here we updated our belief using a single test result (⊕). When diagnosing or classifying with many observed features at once—like words in an email—the Naïve Bayes Algorithm applies this same Bayes rule by assuming features are conditionally independent given the class.
